Anxiety Disorders

New, severe, or unexplained physical symptoms (such as severe chest pain, major breathing difficulty, or fainting) require immediate medical assessment.

Anxiety Center & Education

Understanding Anxiety & Physical Symptoms

Evidence-based insights into how anxiety activates the nervous system, why worry causes real physical discomfort, and how compassionate treatment helps restore peace.

Meet Your Body’s Alarm System

Your brain and body are a team, constantly communicating through electrical nerve pathways and stress hormones such as adrenaline, noradrenaline, and cortisol.

When your brain perceives a potential threat, it turns on an emergency response known as fight, flight, or freeze. Your heart races, your muscles tense, and your breathing speeds up to prepare you to react.

The Smoke Alarm Analogy:
Imagine a smoke detector that goes off because toast got a little too brown. The siren is loud and very real, even though the house is not on fire. Anxiety is like an extra-sensitive alarm system sounding an alert when you are facing a challenge rather than actual physical danger.
Somatic Signs

A Map of Anxiety’s Physical Symptoms

Different parts of your body respond to the same alarm signal. These sensations are physically real — not something you are imagining or making up.

Head & Face

Tension headaches, dizziness, feeling hot or flushed, blushing, dry mouth, and an unconsciously clenched jaw or tight neck muscles.

Chest & Heart

A pounding or fluttering heart rate, rapid pulse, tight sensations across the chest, and the feeling that you cannot take a satisfying, deep breath.

Stomach & Bowels (Gut-Brain)

Nerves and the digestive tract communicate directly. Anxiety causes “butterflies,” nausea, belly cramps, frequent bathroom urgency, or appetite loss.

Hands, Legs & Skin

Sweaty palms, trembling or shaky fingers, cold extremities, restless legs, muscle twitching, or sudden chills and goosebumps.

Dizziness & Breathing

Rapid or shallow breathing (hyperventilation) lowers carbon dioxide in the blood, causing lightheadedness, tingling fingers, and faint feelings.

Feeling Detached (Derealization)

During peak alarm, some individuals feel as though the world is dreamlike, surreal, or as if observing themselves from far away.

Child & Teen Guidance

How Anxiety Looks Different in Children & Teens

Children rarely have the vocabulary to say “I feel clinically anxious.” Frequently, their bodies speak first.

Common Childhood Clues:

  • Recurrent stomachaches or nausea before school, tests, or activities
  • Morning headaches or complaints of muscle tension
  • Tearfulness, excessive clinginess, or bedtime stalling
  • Irritability or sudden meltdowns when facing transitions
  • Frequent school nurse visits without a clear infection

What Adults Can Say & Do:

Validate the feeling: “I believe that your tummy hurts. Sometimes worry can make our bodies hurt. Let’s check what you need and work on it together.”

What to avoid: Do not accuse a child of faking or punish somatic symptoms. Maintain supportive routines, communicate with teachers, and encourage gradual, brave steps rather than complete avoidance.

Practical Self-Regulation

Actionable Coping Skills When the Alarm Goes Off

Evidence-based grounding, breathing resets, and structured steps to settle an activated nervous system.

The 4-Step Plan

When Alarm Activates

  1. Notice: “My hands are shaky. My heart is beating fast.”
  2. Name: “This might be my body alarm. I am safe right now.”
  3. Support: Plant feet, soften shoulders, breathe gently.
  4. Choose: Pick one tiny, manageable next action.
Sensory Reconnection

5-4-3-2-1 Grounding

  • 5 things you can see around you
  • 4 things you can physically feel
  • 3 sounds you can hear
  • 2 scents you can smell
  • 1 taste or taking a slow sip of cool water
Physiological Reset

Gentle Breathing Reset

Do not force huge gulps of air or hold your breath uncomfortably. Sit comfortably with supported feet. Breathe in gently through your nose, then let the air out slowly and easily.

“I can slow down. I do not have to fight my breathing.”

Medical Safety & When to Seek Urgent Evaluation

Emergency Medical Warning: Never assume every physical sensation is anxiety. Seek immediate emergency evaluation (call 911 or go to the nearest emergency room) for:

  • Severe, crushing, or newly occurring chest pain or pressure
  • Major breathing difficulty, blue lips, or sudden severe confusion
  • Fainting (syncope), seizure activity, or one-sided body weakness
  • Thoughts of self-harm or acute crisis (Call or text 988)

Alternative Medical Explanations: Conditions like asthma, cardiac arrhythmias, thyroid disorders, anemia, dehydration, prescription side effects, and caffeine excess can mimic or aggravate anxiety. A physician should evaluate recurring somatic complaints.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does it take for anxiety medication to work?

It depends on the medication class. Certain acute options work quickly, while standard maintenance medications (such as SSRIs) typically take 4–6 weeks of consistent use to demonstrate full therapeutic benefit.


Can anxiety be completely cured?

Anxiety is a natural biological warning system that everyone experiences. However, clinical anxiety disorders can be effectively managed so symptoms no longer disrupt school, work, sleep, or personal vitality.


Do I need psychotherapy to receive psychiatric medication?

While evidence strongly shows combining psychiatric medication with cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) delivers the best long-term outcomes, our providers provide specialized standalone medication management as well.
